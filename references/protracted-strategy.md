# Protracted Strategy (论持久战 + 战略战术)

Sources:《论持久战》(On Protracted War, 1938),《中国革命战争的战略问题》(Problems of Strategy in China's Revolutionary War, 1936)

---

## Principle (原理)

Long-term contests unfold in distinct phases. Matching strategy to the current phase — and knowing when phases shift — is the key to prevailing in extended competitions.

Core concepts:

- **持久战三阶段 (Three phases of protracted contest)**:
  1. **战略防御 (Strategic defensive)**: The opponent is stronger. Preserve strength, avoid decisive engagements, build capability.
  2. **战略相持 (Strategic stalemate)**: Forces are roughly balanced. Consolidate gains, build alliances, prepare for the shift.
  3. **战略反攻 (Strategic offensive)**: Conditions favor action. Press advantages decisively.

- **战略上藐视敌人，战术上重视敌人 (Strategically despise the enemy, tactically respect the enemy)**: Maintain confidence in the long-term outcome (strategic optimism) while treating each concrete step with full seriousness and preparation (tactical rigor).

- **以时间换空间 (Trade time for space)**: When at a disadvantage, use time as an asset. Build strength incrementally rather than seeking premature decisive confrontation.

- **集中优势兵力 (Concentrate superior force)**: Even when globally weaker, create local superiority at the point of engagement. Win a series of small victories rather than gambling on one large one.

**Key insight**: Impatience is the primary failure mode in protracted contests. Understanding which phase the contest is in prevents both premature action and unnecessary passivity.

---

## When to Apply

- Multi-year projects or initiatives
- Competing against a larger, better-resourced opponent
- Organizational transformation or culture change
- Market entry as a small player against established incumbents
- Career development and skill building
- Any situation where "winning slowly" is viable and "winning quickly" is not

---

## How to Apply (Step-by-Step)

### Step 1: Assess the Current Phase

Diagnose honestly which phase the contest is in:

| Phase | Signs | Key Metric |
|-------|-------|------------|
| **Strategic defensive** | Opponent has clear advantages; resources are limited; the terrain is still being learned | Survival and learning rate |
| **Strategic stalemate** | Neither side has decisive advantage; positions are established; competition is ongoing | Steady improvement and consolidation |
| **Strategic offensive** | Momentum has shifted; opportunities are appearing; opponent shows weakness | Rate of progress and market/mind share |

**Critical**: Misjudging the phase is the most common error. Being in stalemate while acting as if in offensive leads to overextension. Being in offensive while acting as if in defensive leads to missed opportunities.

### Step 2: Match Strategy to Phase

**During strategic defensive (战略防御)**:
- Preserve core resources — do not gamble on high-risk plays
- Invest in learning — understand the competitive landscape deeply
- Build foundational capabilities that will compound over time
- Accept short-term losses that enable long-term positioning
- Avoid direct confrontation with the opponent's strengths

**During strategic stalemate (战略相持)**:
- Consolidate gains from the previous phase
- Build alliances and partnerships (see United Front)
- Improve operational efficiency
- Identify the conditions that will trigger the shift to offensive
- Conduct small probes to test opponent weaknesses

**During strategic offensive (战略反攻)**:
- Move decisively — stalemate conditions do not last forever
- Concentrate resources at points of maximum leverage
- Expand scope deliberately but without overextension
- Convert tactical victories into strategic positions

### Step 3: Apply Strategic Contempt + Tactical Respect

For the overall contest:
- **Strategic confidence**: "We will succeed in the long run because [specific structural advantages]."
- **No strategic panic**: Short-term setbacks do not invalidate the long-term direction.

For each specific engagement:
- **Tactical seriousness**: Prepare thoroughly for every concrete step.
- **No tactical arrogance**: Treat every task, every competitor move, every deadline with full respect.
- **Detailed planning**: The difference between strategy and tactics is the level of detail.

### Step 4: Concentrate Superior Force Locally

Even when globally weaker, create local superiority:
- Pick battles where strengths align with the opponent's weaknesses
- Focus resources on a narrow front rather than spreading thin
- Win a series of small decisive victories rather than pursuing one large gamble
- Each local victory builds momentum and shifts the global balance

### Step 5: Monitor for Phase Transitions

Set explicit triggers for reassessment:
- "If [metric] reaches [threshold], reassess whether we have entered stalemate/offensive"
- "Every [time period], evaluate whether the balance of forces has shifted"
- "If [event] occurs, immediately reassess the phase"

Phase transitions are often gradual — the stalemate does not announce itself. Regular assessment prevents strategic lag.

## Modern Example

**Scenario**: A 10-person startup enters an enterprise software market dominated by a well-funded incumbent with 500+ customers.

**Phase assessment**: Strategic defensive — the incumbent has brand recognition, customer relationships, and 50x the engineering team.

**Strategy**:

**Defensive phase (Year 1)**:
- Do not try to compete head-to-head on features
- Pick 2-3 underserved customer segments the incumbent ignores (concentrate superior force locally)
- Build deep relationships with these segments — become indispensable to them
- Learn the market deeply through these customers (investigation method)
- Strategic contempt: "Their architecture is from 2015; our modern stack will be their weakness"
- Tactical respect: "Every customer meeting, every feature, every demo — prepare obsessively"

**Stalemate phase (Year 2-3)**:
- Expand from initial segments into adjacent ones
- Build integrations and partnerships that the incumbent lacks
- Start appearing in competitive evaluations — not winning all, but credible
- Monitor: track win rate in competitive deals as a phase transition indicator

**Offensive trigger**: When win rate in head-to-head evaluations exceeds 40%, shift to offensive — increase sales investment, expand marketing, pursue the incumbent's weaker customer segments.

**Key**: The startup that tries to go offensive in Year 1 burns cash and fails. The one that stays defensive through Year 3 when conditions have shifted misses the window. Phase awareness is the strategic advantage.
