# United Front & Spark (统一战线 + 星星之火)

Sources:《论反对日本帝国主义的策略》(On Tactics Against Japanese Imperialism, 1935),《星星之火，可以燎原》(A Single Spark Can Start a Prairie Fire, 1930),《目前形势和我们的任务》(The Present Situation and Our Tasks, 1947)

---

## Part 1: United Front (统一战线)

### Principle (原理)

In any contest or initiative, people can be divided into three groups: supporters, neutrals, and opponents. The strategy is to **unite with all who can be united, isolate the core opposition, and win over the middle**.

Core concepts:

- **团结一切可以团结的力量 (Unite all forces that can be united)**: Maximize the coalition. Do not demand perfect alignment — find the common ground.
- **争取中间力量 (Win over the middle forces)**: Neutrals are the strategic prize. They determine the balance of power.
- **孤立顽固势力 (Isolate the diehards)**: Do not waste resources trying to convert hardened opponents. Isolate them by winning everyone else.
- **又团结又斗争 (Unity and struggle)**: Alliances are not unconditional. Maintain principled positions while finding practical common ground.

**Key Quotes (引用语录)**:

> "谁是我们的敌人？谁是我们的朋友？这个问题是革命的首要问题。" ——《中国社会各阶级的分析》

> "团结一切可以团结的力量。" ——《论十大关系》

> "利用矛盾，争取多数，反对少数，各个击破。" ——《目前形势和我们的任务》

> "星星之火，可以燎原。" ——《星星之火，可以燎原》

**Key insight**: The initiative does not require unanimous agreement. It requires enough allies to make the opposition irrelevant.

### When to Apply

- Driving organizational change with mixed support
- Cross-team initiatives requiring buy-in from diverse stakeholders
- Introducing new processes, tools, or technologies
- Political navigation within organizations
- Building consensus for a controversial decision

### How to Apply (Step-by-Step)

#### Step 1: Map the Stakeholder Landscape

Classify every relevant stakeholder or group:

| Category | Definition | Typical % |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Core supporters (核心支持者)** | Actively want the initiative to succeed | 10-20% |
| **Lean supporters (倾向支持者)** | Generally favorable but not yet committed | 15-25% |
| **Neutrals (中间力量)** | No strong opinion; will follow the perceived majority | 30-40% |
| **Lean opponents (倾向反对者)** | Have concerns but could be persuaded | 10-20% |
| **Core opponents (核心反对者)** | Actively opposed; unlikely to change | 5-15% |

#### Step 2: Understand Each Group's Interests

For each group, identify:
- What they care about most (their principal contradiction)
- What they fear about the proposal
- What they would gain from the proposal
- What would move them one category toward support

#### Step 3: Build from the Core Outward

Sequence of engagement:

1. **Solidify core supporters** — align on messaging, distribute roles
2. **Convert lean supporters to active supporters** — address their specific concerns, give them ownership
3. **Win over neutrals** — this is where the contest is decided. Show momentum, early wins, and social proof
4. **Engage lean opponents** — address legitimate concerns directly; find compromises that do not sacrifice the core
5. **Isolate core opponents** — do not fight them directly; let them become the minority position naturally

#### Step 4: Create Early Wins

Demonstrate value quickly:
- Find the smallest, most visible success that validates the initiative
- Publicize it — neutrals respond to evidence, not arguments
- Use early adopters as ambassadors to their peers

#### Step 5: Maintain the Coalition

Alliances require maintenance:
- Keep supporters informed and valued
- Deliver on promises to each group
- Address emerging concerns before they escalate
- Accept imperfect alignment — 80% agreement is a strong coalition

## Part 2: A Single Spark (星星之火，可以燎原)

### Principle (原理)

**星星之火，可以燎原** — A single spark can start a prairie fire.

Large-scale change does not require large-scale resources at the start. A focused, well-placed small effort can create disproportionate impact — if the conditions are right and the effort is sustained.

Core concepts:

- **小处着手 (Start from a small place)**: Begin where conditions are most favorable, not where the problem is biggest.
- **以点带面 (Use the point to lead the surface)**: Success in one area creates a model that spreads to others.
- **星火必须燎原 (The spark must become a fire)**: Starting small is only the beginning. The spark must be nurtured into sustained momentum — otherwise it dies.
- **条件成熟 (Conditions must be ripe)**: A spark in wet grass dies. Assess whether conditions support the spread before investing.

### When to Apply

- Very limited resources but ambitious goals
- Need to prove a concept before getting buy-in
- Introducing innovation in a conservative environment
- Starting a movement or culture change
- Building a new product or team from scratch

### How to Apply (Step-by-Step)

#### Step 1: Assess Whether Conditions Are Ripe

Before starting, check:
- Is there latent demand or frustration that the initiative addresses?
- Is there at least one environment where conditions are favorable?
- Can a small success be visible enough to inspire others?

If conditions are not ripe, invest in creating them rather than forcing the spark.

#### Step 2: Choose the Ignition Point

Select the starting point based on:
- **Highest probability of success** (not highest impact)
- **Most visible to potential allies** (neutrals need to see it work)
- **Most transferable** (the lesson must generalize)

#### Step 3: Execute with Full Commitment

The initial effort must succeed. Concentrate resources on this one point:
- Assign the best people available
- Over-invest in the pilot
- Remove every obstacle possible
- Treat the pilot as the most important initiative in the portfolio

#### Step 4: Spread Deliberately

Once the spark catches:
- Document the success — what worked, what was learned, what is replicable
- Find the next 2-3 ignition points that are ready
- Send ambassadors from the first success to the new locations
- Adapt the approach to local conditions (具体问题具体分析)

#### Step 5: Sustain the Fire

Momentum requires continuous fuel:
- Celebrate and communicate each new success
- Build infrastructure that makes the new way easier than the old way
- Transition from hero-driven effort to systematic process

## Modern Example

**Scenario**: A senior engineer wants to introduce automated testing culture in an organization where only 20% of code has tests and leadership is skeptical of the ROI.

**Stakeholder map**:
- Core supporters: 3 engineers who already write tests (10%)
- Lean supporters: 5 engineers who have seen testing work at previous jobs (17%)
- Neutrals: 12 engineers with no strong opinion (40%)
- Lean opponents: 7 engineers who see tests as slowing down delivery (23%)
- Core opponents: 3 senior devs who actively resist ("we've shipped fine without tests") (10%)

**United Front strategy**:
1. Solidify the 3 core supporters — form an informal testing guild
2. Convert the 5 lean supporters — pair program with them, help them write their first tests
3. Win neutrals — present data from the pilot (see Spark below)
4. Engage lean opponents — address their speed concern by showing tests catch bugs that currently take 2 days to debug
5. Isolate core opponents — do not argue publicly; let the evidence accumulate

**Spark strategy**:
1. Conditions: multiple recent production incidents from untested code (latent frustration exists)
2. Ignition point: Pick the service with the most recent outage. Add test coverage to its most critical path. Show it catches the exact type of bug that caused the outage.
3. Execute: Testing guild spends 2 weeks building exemplary test coverage for one service
4. Spread: Present results at engineering all-hands. Offer to help 2 more teams. Create testing templates and a "first test" guide.
5. Sustain: Add test coverage to PR review checklist. Set up CI enforcement for new code. After 6 months, testing is the default, not the exception.
